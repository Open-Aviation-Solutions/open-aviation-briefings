# Learning objectives for this part

By the end of this session, our aim is to be able to:

- **Explain** when to plan an alternate, holding fuel or extra fuel, and **identify** the decision points on a route
- **Describe** how to fly for maximum range and for maximum endurance, and when to use each
- **Plan** and **fly** a diversion: decide, estimate, refine, tell
- **Describe** how to navigate at low level and in reduced visibility, and when to turn back
- **Apply** the lost procedure to re-establish position
- **State** the actions for radio failure, turbulence and an engine failure en route

And then later in our actual flight: we'll fly each of these as a scenario along today's route.

# Options are planned on the ground

On the first navigation flight we planned **where we'd go**. Today we also plan **where we'd go instead**:

| Plan for | Because |
| -------- | ------- |
| A **destination alternate** | The forecast at the destination may not be good enough to land |
| **Holding fuel** | An INTER or TEMPO in the forecast may mean waiting it out |
| **Additional fuel** | Headwinds, a diversion, a longer route home, or simply a margin you choose |
| **En-route alternates** | Aerodromes near track you could reach if the weather, fuel or daylight runs short |

> A diversion you planned on the ground takes seconds in the air. One you didn't can take minutes you don't have.

<!--

CASA NAV.1(b): select a suitable route and altitude considering weather, terrain, airspace, NOTAMs and alternate landing areas; NAV.1(f): complete a flight plan to the planned destination and alternates. Long-brief topic: pre-flight operational decision making — carrying additional fuel, planning for holding and alternate aerodromes.

From the workbook: for a VFR flight, an alternate is needed when the destination forecast is below the alternate minima (cloud ceiling and visibility), or when there's no forecast. An INTER or TEMPO below those minima can instead be covered by holding fuel for its duration (30 or 60 minutes respectively). Check these against the current CASA VFRG and MOS Part 91 before teaching; the rules were rewritten in 2021 and older material still circulates.

Reader instruction: work one of today's legs on the chart and mark the en-route alternates in pencil. Choose them with the student, not for them.

# Decision points

Pick them on the ground, so the decision in the air is already half made:

- **Turn-back point** — "If the weather at ______ isn't at least ______, we turn back here"
- **Fuel check points** — "At ______ we should have ______ remaining. If not, we divert to ______"
- **Last-light check** — "If we're not past ______ by ______, we land at ______"

**Ask:** on today's route, where would you put a turn-back point, and what would make you use it?

# Range or endurance?

**From your workbook:** range is **distance** per litre of fuel; endurance is **time** per litre.

| | Maximum **range** | Maximum **endurance** |
| - | ----------------- | --------------------- |
| You want | To get **as far as possible** | To stay up **as long as possible** |
| You'd use it | Diverting with fuel getting short; lost and heading for a line feature | Holding for weather to clear; waiting for traffic; waiting for help |
| Speed | The **minimum drag** speed ($V_{MD}$) — adjusted for wind | The **minimum power** speed ($V_{MP}$) — slower again |
| Wind | Matters: **faster** into a headwind, a little slower with a tailwind | Makes **no difference** |

<!--

CASA A3.2: straight and level at maximum range and at maximum endurance; NAV.4: configure the aircraft for holding and maximum range. Long-brief topic: calculating and flying for range and endurance.

FIM Ch 16 background: range is flown at the IAS for the best lift/drag ratio, which in practice is one IAS for a light aeroplane type; endurance at the speed where the engine needs the least power to hold height, which the manufacturer recommends. Wind has no effect on endurance, since endurance is about time in the air, not distance over the ground.

PHAK Ch 11 (Aircraft Performance) covers range and endurance performance. Ask the student for the workbook answers before revealing the wind row.

-->

# Setting it up in the aeroplane

Check your aircraft's flight manual for its **range** and **endurance** figures before the flight. Then in the air:

- **Power** — set the flight manual's range or endurance power setting
- **Attitude** — hold height as the speed settles; **trim**
- **Mixture** — **lean** per the flight manual. It's a big part of the saving
- **Carburettor heat** — cold unless you need it
- **Level** — the flight manual's range chart shows which level gives the best range; a better wind at another level may beat it

> Range and endurance are configurations you set up, not speeds you hope for. **Power, attitude, trim — then lean.**

<!--

CASA A3.2 (select power, attitude and configuration; balance and trim accurately). FIM Ch 16 background on the engine side: for a normally aspirated engine and fixed-pitch propeller, the flight manual's cruise and range tables decide the best level; the student should know where those tables are and how to read them. Lean mixture and cold carburettor heat whenever possible. Consider climbing or descending to reduce a headwind or use a tailwind.

Reader instruction: fill in your aircraft's range and endurance power settings and speeds on the student's kneeboard card before the flight.

# Why would we divert?

**Ask** — before revealing: name four reasons you might need to land somewhere other than where you planned.

- **Weather** — worse than forecast on the route or at the destination
- **Fuel** — headwinds or delays have eaten into the margin
- **Daylight** — running out before you'd get there
- **The aeroplane** — a rough engine, a partial failure, a warning light
- **You** — or a passenger: illness, fatigue, discomfort

> The hardest part of a diversion is **deciding to do it**. Decide early, while you still have options.

<!--

It shows up in the ATSB's VFR-into-IMC reports again and again: pilots press on because they've already come so far.

- **Fly the aeroplane first** — a diversion is a busy moment

# Flying a diversion: decide, estimate

> *Decide* — "I'm diverting to ______."
>
> ***Estimate*** — **turn now**, onto a rough heading towards it. Log the time.

- Estimate the track by eye on the chart; use what you've learned about the wind on this flight to allow for drift
- A turn onto roughly the right heading **now** beats an exact heading in five minutes' time

# Flying a diversion: decide, estimate, refine

> *Decide* — "I'm diverting to ______."
>
> *Estimate* — turn now, onto a rough heading. Log the time.
>
> ***Refine*** — draw the track, then measure the **track**, **distance**, **heading**, **ETA** and **fuel**.

- Track and distance: a protractor and scale rule, or your thumb and the 10° drift lines as a guide
- Heading: the drift you've been allowing for on this flight; 1-in-60 to correct the first estimate
- **Check the fuel and the daylight** against the new ETA — do they still work?

<!--

CASA NAV.7(d): revise flight plan considering weather, terrain, airspace and fuel available. The student's Lesson 01 skills (1-in-60, drift, ETA) do all the work here; this is them under time pressure.

Thumb technique: on a 1:250,000 VNC a thumb width is a useful rough distance, so measure your own thumb against the scale rule before the flight. Reader instruction: have the student measure theirs during planning.

# Flying a diversion: decide, estimate, refine, tell

> *Decide* — "I'm diverting to ______."
>
> *Estimate* — turn now, onto a rough heading. Log the time.
>
> *Refine* — track, distance, heading, ETA, fuel.
>
> ***Tell*** — ATS (or whoever holds your SARWATCH): your diversion, your new ETA, and an **amended SARTIME**.

- Then **fly it** like any other leg: gross error check, a fix at the first checkpoint, revise the ETA
- If fuel is the reason — fly for **range**. If you'll have to wait at the other end — **endurance**

# When the cloud comes down

The trap is gradual: a little lower, a little lower, and then **no way back**.

- **Set your limits before you go** — the lowest cloud base and visibility you'll accept, and where you'll turn back
- **Turn back early** — turn while you can still see the way back clearly; a 180° turn is the safest manoeuvre you have
- **Don't descend into lower ground** to stay under cloud — the terrain rises to meet it
- **Never fly into cloud.** Without an instrument rating, that's the classic fatal outcome

> **Turn back while it's still an option.**

<!--

# The bad-weather configuration

If you must fly in reduced visibility on the way out of it, **slow down**:

- **Speed** — a lower speed gives more time to see, and a tighter turn radius if you need to turn back
- **Flap** — as the flight manual recommends for low-speed cruise
- **Lights** — landing and strobe lights on, to be seen
- **Lookout** — for terrain, wires, towers and other aircraft also escaping the weather

Check your aircraft's flight manual for a **reduced-visibility** or **precautionary** configuration — you flew it in Lesson 24.

# Navigating at low level

Same method — time, map, ground — but **faster**, and you **see less**:

| At normal height | At low level |
| ---------------- | ------------ |
| Wide view, features in plan | Narrow view, features in **profile** |
| Checkpoints every 10–20 NM | More checkpoints, closer together; time marks every **2 minutes** |
| Towns, lakes, roads | **Towers, silos, spot heights**, the shape of a hill |
| Plenty of time for the log | Few log entries — **flying the aeroplane comes first** |

- Checkpoint overdue? **Assume you passed it**, and look for the next
- Never below **500 ft AGL**; clear of built-up and noise-sensitive areas

<!--

Required at standard 3 this lesson: introduced, not yet consistent.

# The hazards down low

- **Wires and towers** — often invisible until too late. Study the chart for them, and expect them across valleys and near roads
- **Wind and turbulence** — stronger effect close to the ground; the drift you *see* is greater, and so is the illusion of skidding in a downwind turn
- **Rising ground** — a valley that narrows, or cloud that meets the hills
- **Other aircraft** — also staying low under the cloud
- **No margin for an engine failure** — less time, fewer fields in reach

> Low level is not a shortcut. It's a **tool for getting out of a problem**, used with care.

# Unsure — or lost?

There's a big difference between:

- **Unsure of position** — you have a dead reckoning position from your log; you just haven't confirmed it on the ground
- **Lost** — you have no idea where you are

> Act at the first sign of **uncertainty**, so it never becomes **lost**.

<!--

CASA NAV.6(a): acknowledge positional uncertainty in a timely manner. FIM Ch 16 background: the main cause of getting lost is human error, and there's a big difference between being uncertain of your true position while knowing your DR position, and being completely lost. The log from Lesson 01 is what keeps a pilot in the first category.

Normalise it: almost every student becomes unsure of position at some point in navigation training. That's an opportunity to practise this procedure, not a failure.

-->

---

# The lost procedure: admit it

> ***Admit it*** — "I'm unsure of my position." Say it out loud.

- **Hold your heading** — don't wander towards things that look familiar
- **Fly the aeroplane**, and keep the lookout going

<!--

As with the diversion, the procedure is built up over the next four slides. Have the student say each step back before revealing the next.

The commonest mistake is not admitting it: carrying on, turning towards look-alikes, and making the problem worse with every minute.

-->

---

# The lost procedure: admit it, check

> *Admit it* — "I'm unsure of my position."
>
> ***Check*** — what was the **last positive fix**, and what have you flown since?

- **DI** aligned with the compass?
- **Variation and drift** applied the right way?
- The **track** on the map matches the plan?
- Nothing near the **compass** — a phone, a headset case, a camera?

<!--

CASA NAV.6(c): apply a recognised method to re-establish aircraft position. FIM Ch 16 background: the first action is to decide on the last positive fix and check the headings steered since, checking the DI against the compass, the application of variation and drift, the track on the map against the flight plan, and interference with the compass.

These four checks find the cause of most "lost" situations: a gross error, not bad map reading. A DI that's precessed, or variation applied the wrong way, gives a steady, puzzling track error.

-->

---

# The lost procedure: admit it, check, search

> *Admit it* — "I'm unsure of my position."
>
> *Check* — last positive fix, and what you've flown since.
>
> ***Search*** — work out your **DR position**, draw a **circle of uncertainty** around it, and read from **ground to map** inside it.

- Circle radius: about **10%** of the distance flown since the last fix
- Look for the **big** features first — a coastline, a lake, a railway, a range of hills — and check **two or more** before believing a fix
- If you're nearing the ETA for a checkpoint, carry on for about **10%** more of the time since the last fix

<!--

CASA NAV.6(c, d): apply recognised method to re-establish position; fix position. FIM Ch 16 background: establish a circle of uncertainty centred on the DR position with a radius of 10% of the distance from the last fix; if en route, continue to the next checkpoint while map reading from ground to map; at ETA, continue for a further 10% of the time since the last fix. When a position seems established, double-check every feature: one feature alone may not establish it, but two or more within a reasonable time can.

Ground to map, not map to ground: when you're sure of your position, you read map to ground ("I should see a river"). When you're not, you read ground to map ("I see a river bend; where is there one like that inside my circle?").

# The lost procedure: admit it, check, search, act

> *Admit · check · search* — and if you still can't fix your position, ***act***:

- **Ask for help** — ATS can help. Ask **early**, not as a last resort
- **Head for a line feature** — coastline, railway, highway, river
- **Climb**, if cloud and airspace allow, to see further
- **Reverse heading** back towards the last fix
- **Keep the log going**; set up for **range** if fuel is a concern
- If fuel or daylight is running out: a **precautionary search and landing** — while you still have time to do it properly

<!--

Keep the log going: it helps you and anyone trying to help you. Remind the student to work out a safe endurance and to consider flying for maximum range.

# GNSS — a supplement, not a substitute

**From your workbook:** how GNSS works, RAIM, and its limits.

- **Before flight** — initialise it, check the database and **RAIM**, load and **check** the flight plan
- **In flight** — use it to **confirm** a fix, not to find one; compare it with the map
- **When it disagrees with the map** — find out why before you believe either one
- **When you're unsure of position** — it's a great tool to get a fix. Use it, then work out why you were off track

> Fly the aeroplane, navigate by the map and the clock — and let the GNSS check your work.

# Summary

| Topic | Key points |
| ----- | ---------- |
| Planning for options | Alternate, holding, extra fuel, en-route alternates; decision points set on the ground |
| Range and endurance | Range: $V_{MD}$, faster into wind. Endurance: $V_{MP}$, wind irrelevant. Power, attitude, trim, lean |
| Diversion | **Decide · estimate · refine · tell** — then fly it like any leg |
| Weather | Set limits before you go; turn back early; never into cloud |
| Low level | Slow down; more features, in profile; assume an overdue feature is passed; ≥ 500 ft AGL |
| Lost procedure | **Admit · check · search · act** — circle of uncertainty, ground to map, ask early |
| Emergencies | Radio: 7600, ERSA. Turbulence: speed, attitude. Engine: aviate, navigate, communicate |
