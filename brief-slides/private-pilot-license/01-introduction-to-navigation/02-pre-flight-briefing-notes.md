# Using these notes

The long brief is done and the student has planned today's route — this briefing reviews it, it doesn't add anything new. CASA allows about **30 minutes**, longer than earlier lessons, because a navigation flight has more parts: **ask, draw, and listen** rather than present, and have the student's own chart and log on the table throughout.

**The components** (choose your own order on the planning page):

| Component | ~ time |
|-----------|--------|
| The aim of the flight | 2 min |
| Checking the plan | 6–8 min |
| Today's flight — whiteboard walk-through | 8–10 min |
| Threats and how we'll manage them | 4–5 min |
| Airmanship emphasis — workload and lookout | 4 min |
| Questions, then fly | 2 min |

## The aim of the flight

**Ask** — "From the long brief: what are we setting out to do today?"

**Expect** — in the student's own words:

- **navigate** today's route by dead reckoning and map reading — time, map, ground
- **stay clear** of controlled and restricted airspace, operating in Class G
- **depart from and arrive at** non-towered aerodromes, with the right radio calls
- keep the **navigation and fuel log** going, and correct any track error

Write the aim on the board — everything else hangs off *plan, fly, log, arrive*.

---

# Checking the plan

This is where the plan from the long brief gets its final check, together — not re-done.

**Ask** — "Walk me through your log: first leg, heading, time, fuel."

**Expect** — headings on the correct side of track for the wind; times and fuel that make sense against a rough mental estimate.

**Check together:**

- Latest forecasts and NOTAMs — anything changed since the long brief? **Go / no-go:** ______
- Last light: ______ &nbsp;&nbsp; Planned landing back: ______
- Fuel required: ______ &nbsp;&nbsp; Fuel on board: ______
- Flight notification lodged / SARTIME: ______
- ERSA entry for the destination read — runway, circuit direction, CTAF, any special procedures

---

# Today's flight — whiteboard walk-through

**Draw** — the route as a strip map down the board, from the student's chart: departure → each leg → destination → return. Mark on each leg:

- Heading ______ &nbsp; Time ______ &nbsp; Checkpoints ______
- Airspace boundaries and restricted areas near track
- The line features you'll use for timing checks

**Draw** — the arrival at the away aerodrome: inbound call point, overfly or direct join, circuit direction ______, runway ______.

**Ask** — "What will you be doing between checkpoints?"

**Expect** — the work cycle: *time, map, ground*, log the fix, revise the ETA, FREDA, then lookout.

**Ask** — "Who's flying, and who's navigating?"

**Expect** — for the first leg I'll fly the aeroplane and you'll do the navigating: the log, the work cycle and the calls. We'll swap as the workload allows. Agree the plan on the board.

# Threats and how we'll manage them

I'M SAFE and PAVE are standing checks before **every** flight — by now the student runs them, rather than being taught them.

**Ask** — "Run me through your I'M SAFE — any flags today?"

**Expect** — honest self-assessment. A long flight asks for more stamina and concentration than a training-area lesson.

**Ask** — "Apply PAVE to today's flight — what stands out?"

**Expect** — Pilot, Aircraft, enVironment, External. Listen for the **enVironment** angle — weather en route, terrain, airspace near track — and the **External** one: pressure to get there, or back, on time.

**Ask** — apply **TEAM** to each: "What are our options?"

- **Transfer** · **Eliminate** · **Accept** · **Mitigate**

Likely ones to surface: **straying into controlled or restricted airspace** (mitigate — margins marked on the chart, boundaries briefed); **weather worse than forecast en route** (mitigate — a turn-back or diversion point agreed now); **head-down time** (mitigate — the work cycle, and the instructor taking the flying when workload spikes).

---

# Airmanship emphasis — workload and lookout

Navigation is the busiest flying the student has done so far — the risk is being so busy navigating that nobody is **looking out** or **flying the aeroplane**.

**Ask** — "When the workload builds, what comes first?"

**Expect** — *aviate, navigate, communicate*: fly the aeroplane, then the navigation, then the radio. Ask for help early.

**Ask** — "How long should your head be down, at most, at any one time?"

**Expect** — only as long as one step of the cycle takes — then look out, then back in.

**Normalise the surprise** — tell the student plainly: sooner or later a checkpoint *won't* appear when expected. **That's normal on a first navigation flight — and exactly what the clock and the log are for.** Don't hunt for it, don't turn towards anything that looks similar: note the time, carry on with the heading, and look for the next one.

---

# Questions, then fly

**Ask** — "Any questions before we head out?"

Quick recap as you pack up the whiteboard:

- **Time, map, ground** — know what you're looking for before you look
- Log every fix, and **revise the ETA**
- **Aviate first** — and look out between every cycle
- **Cancel the SARTIME** when we're down

Confirm walking out: chart, log, pencil, computer and ERSA in the bag; who flies the first leg: ______.

Go flying.
