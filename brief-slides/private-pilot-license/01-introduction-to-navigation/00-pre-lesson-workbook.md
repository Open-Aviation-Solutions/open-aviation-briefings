# Introduction to Navigation
## Pre-lesson Workbook

_CASA Private Pilot License (Aeroplane) — CASA Sample Syllabus Lesson 01, Pre-lesson Workbook_

Complete this before your lesson — on your own, at your own pace. For each topic: watch a video or read the pages listed, then answer the questions in the space provided. Print this out, or write your answers in a notebook. There's more here than in earlier lessons — navigation brings a lot of new reference material — so allow about 45–60 minutes, and we'll check it together at the start of the theory brief.

# 1. Your charts and documents

Every navigation flight starts at the map table. Australian VFR charts come in different scales for different jobs — planning the big picture, navigating en route, and finding your way near busy airspace — and the ERSA tells you everything about the aerodromes at either end.

**Watch:**
- CASA [Out-n-Back — Episode 1: Introduction and planning](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7L1x_vv9kL4) — CASA's own series following a real VFR navigation flight, starting with the planning.

**Read:**
- CASA [Visual Flight Rules Guide — Charts, p. 117](https://www.casa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-02/visual-flight-rules-guide.pdf#page=129) — which VFR charts exist and what each is for.

**Answer:**

1. Name the three VFR charts you'd use for navigation (other than the planning chart), and the scale of each: ________________________________

2. Which chart would you want when navigating close to controlled airspace around a major city, and why? ________________________________

3. Name three things you'd look up in the ERSA entry for a destination aerodrome: ________________________________

# 2. Airspace on your chart

Your first navigation flights stay in **Class G** airspace — but the chart around you is full of boundaries you must stay clear of unless you have a clearance: controlled airspace steps, and **prohibited**, **restricted** and **danger** areas.

**Read:**
- CASA VFRG — [Airspace classification, p. 220](https://www.casa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-02/visual-flight-rules-guide.pdf#page=232) and [Prohibited, restricted and danger areas, p. 225](https://www.casa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-02/visual-flight-rules-guide.pdf#page=237).

**Answer:**

1. In one sentence each: what's the difference between a **prohibited**, a **restricted** and a **danger** area? ________________________________

2. A restricted area on your route is marked on the VNC. How do you find out whether it's active for today's flight? ________________________________

3. How is the base of a controlled-airspace step shown on the chart, and why does it matter when you choose your cruising level? ________________________________

# 3. Last light, and who comes looking for you

Two times bound every navigation flight: **last light** — you must be on the ground before it without a night rating — and your **SARTIME**, the time search and rescue starts if you haven't told anyone you're safe.

**Read:**
- CASA VFRG — [Daylight and darkness, p. 112](https://www.casa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-02/visual-flight-rules-guide.pdf#page=124), then try the Geoscience Australia [sunrise, sunset and twilight calculator](https://geodesyapps.ga.gov.au/sunrise) for your home aerodrome.
- CASA VFRG — [SARTIME, p. 153](https://www.casa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-02/visual-flight-rules-guide.pdf#page=165) and [Pre-flight briefing and flight notification, p. 163](https://www.casa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-02/visual-flight-rules-guide.pdf#page=175).

**Answer:**

1. Define **last light**. Why is *sunset* the wrong time to plan around? ________________________________

2. What's last light at your home aerodrome on the day of your lesson (in local time *and* UTC)? ________________________________

3. What is a SARTIME, how do you nominate one, and what happens if you forget to cancel it? ________________________________

# 4. Fuel — what you must carry

Running out of fuel is one of the most avoidable accident causes there is. The rules set a **final reserve** you must never plan to use, and a sequence of other fuel components that add up to what you need before you start the engine.

**Watch:**
- CASA [Explaining the rules — Fuel planning](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aVVoCv-ywHc) — the fuel components, and when to declare *minimum fuel* or *MAYDAY fuel*.

**Read:**
- CASA VFRG — [Fuel requirements, p. 98](https://www.casa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-02/visual-flight-rules-guide.pdf#page=110) — the fuel components and the final reserve table.

**Answer:**

1. What is the **final reserve** for a day VFR flight in a light piston-engine aeroplane? ________________________________

2. List the fuel components that make up your planned fuel, in order: ________________________________

3. You planned to land with 45 minutes of fuel remaining, but a headwind has cost you 20 minutes. What should you be thinking about, and what call might you need to make? ________________________________

# 5. Dead reckoning — heading, track, and the 1-in-60 rule

Visual navigation isn't following roads from town to town — it's **dead reckoning**: flying a calculated heading for a calculated time, and using the map to *confirm* where you are. When you find yourself off track, the **1-in-60 rule** turns "how far off" into "how many degrees" in your head.

**Watch (pick one):**
- Flight Club — [1 in 60 rule](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WiXU2OrDeNo) — Australian, short and clear.
- PilotPracticeExams — [1 in 60 Rule Explained For Flight Training](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q3_Nlhj2R0Q) — a slower walk-through with worked examples.

**Read:**
- FAA [Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge, Chapter 16 (Navigation)](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/18_phak_ch16.pdf) — the *Pilotage*, *Dead Reckoning* and *Wind Triangle* sections.

**Answer:**

1. In your own words, what's the difference between **heading** and **track**? What makes them differ? ________________________________

2. You've flown 20 NM and find yourself 2 NM left of track. What's your **track error** in degrees? ________________________________

3. Why is the **clock** (or your watch) one of the most important navigation instruments you have? ________________________________

# 6. Radio calls at non-towered aerodromes

Your first navigation flight lands at an aerodrome with no control tower. Everyone there relies on everyone else making clear, well-timed radio calls on the **CTAF** — and on arriving in a predictable way.

**Watch (pick one):**
- CASA [Explaining the rules — Radio calls](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nW8TeUxE6_E) — short animation of the calls and when to make them.
- Flight Safety Australia — [Radio calls at non-controlled aerodromes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m4M_0Fu_AJ0) — planning your calls for each stage of the flight.
- CASA [Out-n-Back — Episode 7](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1rsM_6BoLHo) — the "golden rules" of radio calls and operations at non-controlled aerodromes, on a real flight.

**Read:**
- CASA [Be heard, be seen, be safe — radio procedures in non-controlled airspace](https://www.casa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-07/avsafety-radio-procedures-in-non-controlled-airspace-card.pdf) — a pocket card worth keeping in your flight bag.
- CASA VFRG — [Non-controlled aerodromes, p. 238](https://www.casa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-02/visual-flight-rules-guide.pdf#page=250) — including the **overfly** procedure for joining at an unfamiliar aerodrome.

**Answer:**

1. Write out your **inbound call** for an aerodrome of your choice, in order: ________________________________

2. When would you use the **overfly** procedure to join, and at what height? ________________________________

3. What transponder code do you squawk in Class G airspace? ________________________________

# Bring this to your lesson

- Bring your completed answers (printed, or your notebook) to the theory brief
- We'll check through them together and answer anything that didn't make sense
- From there, the live theory brief builds on this: in Part 1 we'll plan today's actual route together — weather, chart, fuel and flight plan — and in Part 2, how to fly it: departing on track, the navigation work cycle, correcting track errors, and arriving at a non-towered aerodrome

> Good navigation is mostly done before you start the engine. The better your planning, the more time you'll have in the air to look outside.
