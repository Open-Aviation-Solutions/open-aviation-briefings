# Learning objectives for this part

By the end of this session, our aim is to be able to:

- **Interpret** the forecasts and NOTAMs for a route, and decide whether it can be flown under the VFR
- **Select** a route and prepare the chart — tracks, distances, checkpoints and drift lines
- **Choose** a cruising level, and **calculate** last light for the flight
- **Calculate** the fuel required and prepare the navigation and fuel log
- **Describe** how to lodge a flight plan or nominate a SARTIME

And then: we'll plan today's actual route together, ready to fly after Part 2.

# The planning sequence

Plan in the same order every time, so nothing is missed:

| Step | What you're deciding |
| ---- | -------------------- |
| **1. Weather and NOTAMs** | Can the flight be done under the VFR at all? |
| **2. Route and chart** | Where exactly will we fly, and what will we see? |
| **3. Level and last light** | How high, and by when must we be down? |
| **4. Fuel and log** | How much fuel, and how will we track it? |
| **5. Flight plan and SARTIME** | Who knows where we are, and when to worry? |

> **Estimate first, then calculate.** A rough mental answer catches the big mistakes a careful calculation can hide.

<!--

FIM Ch 16 background: weather, then maps, then the flight plan, then notification — and
insist the student estimates distances, headings, times and fuel *before* measuring or
calculating, as a double check. CASA NAV.1.

-->

# Weather and NOTAMs

- **Area forecast (GAF)** — cloud, visibility and weather along the whole route
- **Aerodrome forecasts (TAF)** and **reports (METAR/SPECI)** — for the departure and destination
- **Wind at your cruising level** — sets your heading, groundspeed, and fuel
- **NOTAMs** — for every aerodrome, the route, and any restricted areas on it

**Ask:** from today's forecast — can we fly this route under the VFR, and what would make us turn back?

<!--

# Choosing the route

A good route isn't always the straight line:

- **Airspace** — stay clear of controlled airspace and active restricted areas; note every boundary near track
- **Terrain** — high ground, and what's below you if the engine stops
- **Checkpoints** — unmistakable features roughly every 10–20 NM, and a **positive fix** at least every 30 minutes (the regulatory minimum)
- **Alternates** — suitable places to land if the weather or fuel doesn't go to plan

**Ask:** looking at today's chart, where would you *not* want to have an engine failure?

<!--

CASA NAV.1(b): select a suitable route and altitude considering weather, terrain,
airspace, NOTAMs and alternate landing areas. CASR 91.273 / MOS 13.02 — when
navigating by visual reference, positively fix the aircraft's position at intervals not
exceeding 30 minutes (VFRG p. 202).

FIM Ch 16: choose unmistakable check features on or near track at regular intervals;
consider the suitability of terrain for a forced landing. PHAK Ch 16 (Navigation) —
Pilotage.

The instructor is choosing the actual route for today (see the in-flight notes) — this
slide is about the student understanding *why* it runs where it does.

# Good checkpoints

The best features are **unique**, **large**, and **easy to see from above**:

| Good | Poor |
| ---- | ---- |
| A town with a distinctive shape, or a river bend | One of many similar farm dams |
| A railway line crossing a highway | A minor road among many |
| A lake, a coastline, a prominent hill | Anything hidden by terrain or trees |

- A **line feature** across your track (railway, river, power line) is a great timing check — it's hard to miss even if you're off track
- At a few thousand feet, a feature's **outline** matters more than its height

> Pick checkpoints you'll *expect at a time*, not ones you hope to spot.

<!--

FIM Ch 16 background on map reading: conspicuousness depends on angle of view,
dimensions and uniqueness; a feature long in one direction and sharply defined in the
other is often most useful; at low level, elevation matters more, higher up the plan
outline does. PHAK Ch 16 — Pilotage.

# Preparing the chart

1. **Draw the track** from departure to each turning point and the destination
2. **Measure** the true track with the protractor, and the distance with the scale rule
3. **Apply variation** to get the magnetic track — East is least, West is best
4. **Mark 10° drift lines** either side of each leg, from the departure and destination ends
5. **Mark distance ticks** along each leg (every 10 NM, or your checkpoints)
6. **Note** the controlled-airspace steps, restricted areas and frequencies along the way

<!--

FIM Ch 16: have the student draw in the tracks, bearing in mind lanes of entry, the
need to avoid controlled, restricted and prohibited airspace, the suitability of
terrain for a forced landing, and the location of checkpoints. The ten-degree drift
lines assist in applying the one-in-sixty rule in flight (Part 2). CASA NAV.1(a):
select and prepare appropriate navigation charts.

"East is least, West is best" — magnetic = true − easterly variation, + westerly
variation. Most of eastern Australia has easterly variation, so magnetic tracks are
numerically less than true.

# From track to heading — the wind

The forecast wind turns the **track** you drew into the **heading** you'll fly:

- **Drift** — the wind pushes you sideways, so you head *into* the wind to hold the track
- **Groundspeed** — a headwind slows you down, a tailwind speeds you up — changing every time and fuel figure
- Use your navigation computer (or app) — but **estimate first**: which side will the heading be, and roughly how many degrees?

**From your workbook:** heading vs track, and the PHAK *Wind Triangle* section.

# Choosing a cruising level

| Factor | What to consider |
| ------ | ---------------- |
| **VFR cruising levels** | By magnetic track: **000°–179° → odd thousands + 500 ft** (3,500, 5,500…); **180°–359° → even thousands + 500 ft** (4,500, 6,500…) |
| **Cloud** | Stay clear of cloud with the required VMC margins |
| **Terrain** | Safe clearance above the highest ground near track |
| **Airspace** | Below controlled-airspace steps along the route |
| **Wind** | A lower or higher level may give a better tailwind or less headwind |

- Below 3,000 ft AMSL (or below 1,500 ft AGL) in Class G, you don't have to fly a specified VFR level

<!--

CASA NAV.1(b); CASR 91.275 — specified VFR cruising levels, and the exceptions below
3,000 ft AMSL or below 1,500 ft AGL in uncontrolled airspace (VFRG p. 217). The VFRG
also notes that VFR levels give only 500 ft separation from crossing IFR traffic — fly
them accurately. FIM Ch 16: factors are wind, cloud, terrain clearance and conforming
with cruising levels.

Reader instruction: check the VFR cruising levels figure in the VFRG (p. 217) against
this table.

-->

# Last light — and a margin

**From your workbook:** last light is the end of **evening civil twilight** — not sunset.

- Find it for the **destination** (and alternates) as well as home — it can differ by several minutes across a long route
- Work in **UTC** on the flight plan, and convert carefully
- Plan to be on the ground with a **margin** — headwinds, a diversion, or a slow refuel all eat into it

**Ask:** what time is last light today, and what's the latest we could depart and still be home with the margin?

- In flight, it records **departure time, fixes, revised ETAs**, and **fuel checks** — so you always know where you should be, and what's left

# Flight plan and SARTIME

**From your workbook:** what a SARTIME is, and what happens if it's not cancelled.

- **Lodge** — through NAIPS (or your operator's procedure), with your route, level, endurance and SARTIME
- **SARTIME** — at least enough to cover the flight plus a sensible buffer, not so late that a real delay goes unnoticed for hours
- **Amend** — if you change your route, destination, or SARTIME in flight, tell ATS
- **Cancel** — on arrival, every time. Put it in the shutdown checklist

**Ask:** what time would you nominate as today's SARTIME, and why?

<!--

search and rescue (SAR) purposes. NAV.9(l) / ONTA.4(c): cancel SARWATCH on arrival. VFRG
p. 153 (SARTIME), p. 163 (flight notification).

Reader instruction: follow your operator's procedure for flight notification on

# Summary

| Topic | Key points |
| ----- | ---------- |
| Planning sequence | Weather/NOTAMs → route/chart → level/last light → fuel/log → flight plan/SARTIME |
| Route | Clear of airspace and PRD areas; safe terrain; checkpoints; positive fix ≤ 30 min |
| Chart | Track, distance, variation, 10° drift lines, distance ticks |
| Cruising level | By magnetic track — 000°–179° odd + 500, 180°–359° even + 500 |
| Last light | End of civil twilight — plan with a margin |
| Fuel | Estimate first; final reserve 30 min, never planned to be used |
| SARTIME | Lodge it, amend it, and **cancel it** on arrival |
