# You're the pilot

- You're flying a cross-country in a school aeroplane, on your own.
- The forecast said cloud "improving later". We'll fly it one step at a time, and at each step you decide: **continue, divert, or land**.

# Step 1

- Cruising at 2,500 ft above the ground, 30 minutes from home
- Cloud base is lowering — about 3,500 ft now, and it has been dropping for ten minutes
- Visibility is still good, farmland everywhere, plenty of fuel

**Continue, divert, or land?**

# Step 2

- Cloud base is now about 2,800 ft, so you descend to 2,000 ft to stay clear
- Visibility is down to 8 km, with rain showers ahead
- 22 minutes from home, still plenty of paddocks around you

**Continue, divert, or land?**

---

# Step 3

- Cloud base is about 1,800 ft and you're at 1,200 ft
- Visibility is 5 km, and the ground is rising ahead
- Last light is in about 40 minutes, and the paddocks are getting smaller and fewer

**Continue, divert, or land?**

---

# Step 4

- Cloud base is about 1,000 ft and you're at 700 ft
- Visibility is 3 km, with rain across your track
- Trees and a ridge ahead, the last good paddock is behind you, and home is 15 minutes away — if you can see it

**Continue, divert, or land?**

---

# Which step was your last good option?

- Most of us would say step 3 or 4 — the point where it's obviously bad
- But at step 1 or 2 you had height, visibility, daylight, fuel, fields behind you, and a calm head
- Nothing about the engine changed. Only your **options** did

# Today's flying

Unlike Lesson 17, the engine is still running today — this is about judgement, not an unmistakable failure. The same discipline applies: decide early, while you're still in full control.

A precautionary search and landing is for **anything you're not happy with**, not only cloud and last light:

- Weather closing in, fuel getting tight, or daylight running out
- Oil pressure fluctuating, or another engine indication that isn't right
- A sick or distressed passenger
- Becoming unsure of your position

> The aim today: build the judgement to decide early, and the plan to act on that decision calmly.

<!--

Contrast directly with Lesson 17's hook: there, the trigger (engine failure) is unmistakable; here, the trigger is a judgement call the student has to make themselves. Stress that weather and fuel are only the classic examples — a fluctuating oil pressure or a sick passenger is just as good a reason to land early, and the same sequence applies. The trigger also shapes the real-life plan: a sick passenger or an engine indication argues for the nearest suitable field, quickly, while a deteriorating-weather scenario gives more time to search.

-->

# Learning objectives for this lesson

By the end of this session, our aim is to be able to:

- **Describe** the circumstances that call for a precautionary landing, and why the decision must be made early
- **Configure** the aeroplane for a precautionary/poor-visibility cruise
- **Apply** the field-selection criteria to choose and inspect a suitable landing area
- **Describe** the vital-actions sequence, including the PAN call and passenger brief
- **Revise** the short-field landing technique from Lesson 22

And then later in our actual flight: we'll fly the search, inspection and approach sequence for real, from varying positions and simulated conditions.

# The vital-actions sequence

Once you've decided, the sequence runs in order:

1. **Decide** — early, while still in full control, shift to being a "landing pilot"
2. **Configure** — for a precautionary/poor-visibility cruise
3. **Select** — search the area and choose a field against your WOSSSSET criteria
4. **Communicate** — the **PAN** call
5. **Prepare** — brief the passengers
6. **Inspect** — fly the inspection pattern to confirm the field, keeping it in sight
7. **Land** — the short-field technique, then secure the aircraft

- Imagine you're on a genuine cross-country along today's planned route, not just training close to the aerodrome. What's your trigger — the one sign that would make you decide *now*, not "wait and see"?

<!--

# Configuring for the search

Once you've decided, set the aeroplane up to search safely:

- **Slow, safe cruise** — enough margin above the stall to manoeuvre and look, not so slow you're working hard just to stay in the air
- **Some flap** — improves the view over the nose and lowers your safe manoeuvring speed
- **Height** — low enough to assess the ground, high enough to keep options and height in hand

> Fly the aeroplane first. Configuring, searching and selecting all happen with a hand still on the controls, not instead of flying.

<!--

CASA A6.4(g): configure aeroplane for conditions. FIM Ch 15: descend to a safe low cruise height and configuration once poor conditions are simulated. Reader instruction: check your aircraft type's recommended low-level search speed and flap setting before the flight.

-->

# Low-flying hazards

Flying low and slow to search and inspect brings its own risks — recap your Lesson 17 **WOSSSSET** field-selection criteria, then add the hazards it doesn't cover:

- **Wires, poles and towers** — often invisible until close; scan the horizon, not just the ground
- **Terrain and turbulence** — expect sink and turbulence in the lee of hills, trees, and buildings
- **Livestock and people** — avoid frightening animals or overflying people at low height
- **Changing groundspeed in the turn** from your workbook — a genuine effect of wind drift, not a fault with the aeroplane; fly the attitude, not the picture

> Low flying earns its own dedicated lookout — the margin for a surprise is smaller down here.

<!--

FIM Ch 15: practise only in approved areas, avoid frightening livestock and annoying people, turning illusions caused by drift near the ground. CASA A6 4(o): low-flying hazards.

-->

# Communicate and prepare — before the first pass

**Communicate** — from your workbook: the **PAN** call to ATC, made as soon as the decision to land is made, and before the first pass. If things get worse in flight, upgrade to **MAYDAY** without hesitation.

# Join and the 500 ft pass

1. **Join on the normal crosswind** at 1000 ft, **trimmed for 80 kt** — and make your **PAN call to ATC** and give your **passenger brief** *before* the first pass
2. **Downwind at 1000 ft** — time the field from threshold to threshold
3. **Extend downwind**, turn base wide of the field, and descend to **500 ft** — wider than the low passes will be
4. **500 ft pass** — offset right of the landing path, **time the field again**, and **assess WOSSSSET again** from this new height. Then **stay at 500 ft**, configured, and fly the circuit at 500 ft

<!--

The crosswind join is labelled on the diagram as the point by which the PAN call and passenger brief must already be done — the student should not be making radio calls or briefing passengers while flying a low pass. If ATC isn't available, make the call on the area frequency or the CTAF as the operator's procedures require.

Reassessing WOSSSSET on the 500 ft pass matters: the field looks different from circuit height than it did from the search height, and this is a low-cost chance to reject it before going lower. The 500 ft pass is deliberately wider than the later passes so the student has more of the field in view and room to turn away. The offsets and heights are illustrative — sanity-check the geometry against the diagram's rendered output, and against your own aircraft and training area, before using this live.

After the 500 ft pass the aeroplane stays configured and the rest of the circuits are flown at 500 ft AGL, which is what CASA expects to see in training. Be clear that this is the practice standard: in a real event the height depends on why you're there — terrain around the field might argue for climbing back to 1000 ft, while a low cloud base might mean staying at or below 500 ft. Also tell the student that, once the problem is resolved, we will probably want to take off again from the same field, so the inspection should consider the departure as well as the landing.

-->

# Timing the field

Fly trimmed at a steady **80 kt**, and count the seconds from threshold to threshold — **twice**, on the 1000 ft downwind and again on the 500 ft pass. Then **average the two** — or read **groundspeed from the GNSS** and use it in place of averaging.

- 1 kt is about 0.5 m/s, so 80 kt is about **40 m/s**
- **10 s ≈ 400 m**
- **20 s ≈ 800 m**

Use **groundspeed**, not airspeed. The two timings are flown in opposite directions, so averaging them cancels most of the wind. If you have a GNSS, its groundspeed already includes the wind, so one timing at that groundspeed is enough.

> You want at least **400 m** for most small aeroplanes — but check your aircraft's manual for its short-field landing distance for the day.

<!--

Timing the pass is how the student works out the field's length (CASA A6.4(f): landing area length adequate for landing). The syllabus doesn't prescribe the method, so this is standard practice rather than a CASA element: fly trimmed at a steady 80 kt and count seconds threshold to threshold, twice — on the 1000 ft downwind and again on the 500 ft pass — then average the two. 1 kt is about 0.5 m/s (strictly 0.51), so 80 kt is about 40 m/s and the count multiplied by 40 gives a quick estimate in metres: 10 s is about 400 m, 20 s about 800 m. Remind the student the speed that matters is groundspeed. The downwind leg and the 500 ft pass are flown in opposite directions, so one has a tailwind (shorter time, field looks shorter) and the other a headwind (longer time, field looks longer) — averaging the two cancels most of the wind effect, which is the main reason for timing it twice. The GNSS groundspeed is an alternative to averaging: it already includes the wind, so one timed pass multiplied by the displayed groundspeed gives the length directly — still confirm the GNSS is serviceable and displaying groundspeed rather than airspeed. If the two timings disagree by much, fly another timed pass before trusting either. About 400 m is the usual minimum for most small aeroplanes, but the aircraft's own flight manual short-field landing distance for the day (with a margin) is the figure to compare against. Check that 80 kt suits your aircraft type's recommended search speed and configuration before the flight.

-->

# Flying the low passes

1. **First low pass — 200 ft**, slightly right of the intended landing path, level from threshold to threshold — check the surface, note drift, look for obstacles in the overshoot area. **Apply full power two-thirds of the way along the runway**, then climb back to **500 ft** once past the far threshold and fly the circuit at 500 ft
2. **Second pass — 50–100 ft**, again to the right, for a closer re-check — and again **full power two-thirds of the way along the runway**
3. Repeat if you're not satisfied — then either fly the **short-field landing**, or go around and choose another field

> Unlike a forced landing, you have power and time — keep the field in sight and use both to inspect properly before you commit.

<!--

FIM Ch 15 background (structure only, not reproduced): descend to about 500 ft, simulate poor weather, choose a strip, fly over slightly to the right of the intended landing path at about 100 ft to check surface and drift and note obstacles in the overshoot area, climb to about 500 ft and circuit keeping the field in sight, fly a dummy approach again to the right for a re-check, repeat, then short-field landing or go-around. CASA A6.4(i): select landing area, carry out an inspection and assess its suitability — unobstructed approach and overshoot paths, landing area length adequate for landing, surface suitable for aeroplane type and clear of hazards.

Full power two-thirds of the way along the runway is a safety margin on both low passes: the power is already in, so the aeroplane is set to climb away if a hazard appears or the aeroplane sinks, rather than relying on a late decision near the far end. It is not used on the timed 500 ft pass, because the extra power would change the speed before the far threshold and spoil the timing. The pass heights (200 ft, then 50–100 ft) and the ground track are illustrative — sanity-check the geometry against the diagram's rendered output, and against your own aircraft and training area, before using this live.

-->

# Land — short-field revision

The approach was already flown as the last path of the inspection pattern — now the landing itself, the short-field technique from Lesson 22:

- **Full flap** and a stable approach on the short-field profile
- **Minimum recommended speed** crossing the boundary
- **Minimal float**, then **maximum braking**
- **After landing:** secure the aircraft, manage your passengers, and update ATS or other agencies of your situation if you're able to

> If anything about the approach isn't right — go around. You have power; use it.
