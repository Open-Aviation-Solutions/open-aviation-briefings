# It isn't hypothetical

Between 2000 and 2010 the ATSB looked at engine problems during and after take-off in Australian light single-engine aircraft:

- A **partial** power loss was about **three times more likely** than a complete failure
- **Nine** fatal accidents followed the response to a partial power loss — **none** followed a complete failure

> The engine rarely kills anyone. What the pilot does next decides the outcome — and that can be planned before the take-off.

<!--

Source: ATSB, Avoidable Accidents No. 3 — Managing partial power loss after take-off in single-engine aircraft (AR-2010-055), introduction. The student has read the key messages in workbook topic 4, so ask them for the three strategies before revealing them later in the deck.

The point of the slide is that this is a *decision* problem far more than a flying-skill problem: the fatal outcomes were losses of control during a response — mostly turning back — not the engine stopping. That is exactly what a pre-take-off plan and a practised sequence fix.

-->

# Learning objectives for this lesson

By the end of this session, our aim is to be able to:

- **Recognise** a take-off abnormality and **state** the actions for a rejected take-off
- **Deliver** a pre-take-off safety brief that plans for an engine failure before it happens
- **Recall** the actions for an engine failure after take-off, in order, and **explain** why we land ahead rather than turn back
- **Describe** how the options change when the engine fails elsewhere in the circuit
- **Explain** the factors that affect glide performance, and **describe** how to fly and correct a glide approach

And then later in our actual flight: we'll fly each of these as a simulated exercise — the first ones demonstrated, then yours — so the sequence starts to become automatic.

# Recall — the go-around (Lesson 8)

From Lesson 8: when an approach or landing isn't working, we go around — **Power · Attitude & Flap · Trim**, then climb away on the centreline, flying the aeroplane before the radio.

Today, the first word of that is gone. **Without power there's no go-around** — so every decision has to be made earlier, and the approach has to be flown right the first time.

> Same priority as Lesson 8 — **Aviate, Navigate, Communicate** — but with no second attempt.

# Something's not right on the roll

| Cue | What it might mean |
|---|---|
| **RPM low** at full throttle | Not full power — carburettor ice, mixture, a mechanical fault |
| **Slow acceleration** — ASI slow to come alive | Low power, a dragging brake, a soft surface, a blocked pitot |
| **Rough running**, low oil pressure | Fuel, ignition or ice — or engine distress |
| A **door** pops open, or the **centreline** won't hold | Distraction — or a tyre, brake or crosswind problem |

> Check the **RPM** and the **ASI** early in every take-off roll — they're your two best warnings.

<!--

CASA A2.2(x): recognise take-off abnormalities and take appropriate action to reject take-off (can be simulated); A2.2(q): monitor power controls, settings and instruments during take-off.

The static (full-throttle) RPM is the first thing to check — the student should know the expected figure for the type, the same way they'd check it at the run-up. Check the flight manual for the full-throttle static RPM range of your training aircraft.

The open door is worth naming explicitly: in most light aircraft it is noisy and alarming but not dangerous, and the danger comes from trying to close it during the take-off or at low level. On the runway, reject; in the air, fly a normal circuit and land.

# The rejected take-off

On the ground, the answer is simple — **close the throttle and stop**:

1. **Throttle** — closed
2. **Straight** — keep straight on the centreline with rudder
3. **Brakes** — as needed to stop in the runway remaining (back on the control column for nose-wheel types)
4. Then, once stopped: if the engine is the problem, **shut it down** and make the aircraft safe; **advise** the traffic

Decide **before** you roll where the last point to stop is — if you're not at the speed you expect by then, reject.

> Hesitation is what turns a rejected take-off into a runway overrun. If in doubt on the ground, **close the throttle**.

<!--

CASA A2.2(x). The decision point: one widely taught rule of thumb is the 50/70 rule from workbook topic 2 — if the aeroplane hasn't reached about 70% of its lift-off speed by the time half the runway has gone, reject. It's a guide for long runways rather than a precise figure, but it gives the student something concrete to look for. Ask what they found and where.

The ATSB booklet in workbook topic 4 records 24 rejected take-offs in its partial-power sample with no serious or fatal injuries — the cheapest emergency there is, as long as it's decided early.

Check your aircraft's flight manual for its rejected take-off / engine failure during take-off roll checklist — some include mixture to idle cut-off and fuel off once stopped.

-->

# Plan it before you roll — the safety brief

Before every take-off, at the holding point, brief yourself (and any passenger) on what you'll do. For example:

> *"If an engine failure or malfunction occurs during the take-off roll, I'll close the throttle and stop. If it occurs after take-off with runway remaining, I'll land on the runway ahead. If it occurs without runway remaining, I'll lower the nose, glide at* ______ *kt and land within about 30° of the nose. I will not turn back below* ______ *ft."*

- Make it **specific to today** — the runway, the wind, and what lies beyond the end of it
- Look at the ground beyond the runway end **before** you need it

<!--

CASA A2.1(i, j): correctly identify critical airspeeds, configurations, and emergency and abnormal procedures; work out a plan of action, in advance, to ensure the safest outcome in the event of abnormal operations. NTS2 4(d): developing and implementing plans of action to mitigate threats. The ATSB's first strategy in the workbook reading is exactly this: pre-flight decision-making and planning for emergencies at the particular aerodrome.

The wording above is a common Australian form, not a CASA script — the student should make it their own, but it must cover the roll, runway remaining, no runway remaining, and the turn-back height. Fill in the blanks with your training aircraft's best glide speed and your operator's minimum turn-back height (if it has one). Some operators add the speed at which the take-off is rejected, or a partial-power plan — see the partial power slide.

From today the student says this brief, out loud, before every take-off. It's also the first thing a pre-solo check looks for — the FIM expects a student to be proficient at the pre take-off safety brief before first solo.

# Why the nose must come down — now

<four-forces class="right large" height="460px" v_ne="188" v_no="163" v_1="68" cruise-kts="110"></four-forces>

In the climb, the aeroplane is at a **high nose attitude** and a **low speed** — held there only by the engine's thrust.

Take the thrust away and the speed starts falling **immediately**. Hold the climb attitude for a few seconds and you're approaching the stall — close to the ground.

> **Lower the nose to the glide attitude** — first, before anything else.

<!--

CASA A6.1(c–e): correctly identify an engine failure after take-off; apply the highest priority to taking action to control the aeroplane; maintain control of the aeroplane. FIM Ch 11 makes the same point — the climb speed is lower than the glide margin you'd have in the cruise, so the gliding attitude has to be taken very quickly.

Demo script for the four-forces component — have the student predict before each step:

1. Set the attitude to a climb (about 10 degrees nose up) and power to full — let the speed settle at a climb speed. Ask: "What will the airspeed do if the engine stops right now?"
2. Bring the power to zero and leave the attitude where it is. Watch the ASI unwind and the thrust arrow vanish — let the student see how few seconds it takes.
3. Lower the attitude to a glide attitude (about 3–5 degrees nose down — TODO: confirm the attitude that holds best glide speed in the component) and watch the speed recover and settle.

The lesson in step 2 is the one to name: the attitude the aeroplane was perfectly happy in a second ago is now the dangerous one. Nothing else in the sequence matters if the nose stays up.

-->

# Engine failure after take-off — the actions

From memory, in order — and only as much as time allows:

1. **Aviate** — **lower the nose** to the glide attitude and hold best glide speed; flap as needed for the landing
2. **Navigate** — choose a landing area **ahead**, within about **30° either side of the nose**; small turns only, **wings level by about 200 ft**
3. **Communicate** — a MAYDAY call, **only if there's time**

If time permits, before touchdown: **change tanks** (or the quick checks: carburettor heat, fuel, mixture), then **fuel off, ignition off, master off** (after the last flap), **doors unlatched**.

> **Fly the aeroplane all the way to the ground.** A controlled arrival in a paddock is survivable; a stall at 200 ft is not.

<!--

CASA A6.1(c–h): identify the failure, apply the highest priority to controlling the aeroplane, maintain control, perform recall actions, perform emergency actions as far as time permits, manoeuvre to achieve the safest possible outcome, advise others (ATS, other aircraft) if time permits. NTS1.4 (set priorities and manage tasks) and NTS1 4(a) communication under non-normal circumstances.

FIM Ch 11 background (not reproduced): the points it expects briefed are control of the aeroplane, speed of action, the gliding speed, the choice of landing area, the height available, the use of flap, and the fuel, ignition, master and hatches. It restricts the amount of turn to the minimum that obstacles dictate, and stresses that any turn increases the rate of descent and the stall speed. The NZ CAA Flight Instructor Guide gives the same Aviate, Navigate, Communicate order, with the MAYDAY last, only if time permits.

The sequence is deliberately short. The difference from a forced landing from the cruise (Lesson 17) is height: here there may be fifteen seconds between the failure and the ground, so the checks and the call are optional extras, and the field is whatever is in front of you. Lesson 17 will expand all of it.

The shutdown: fuel and ignition off reduce the fire risk on impact; the master goes last if the flap is electric; unlatching the doors stops them jamming if the fuselage distorts. Check these against your aircraft's own engine failure after take-off checklist — it wins where it differs.

-->

# Why not turn back?

Your workbook asked you to reason this out first. The short version:

- A **180° turn isn't enough** — you need well over 180° of turning, plus the distance to get back to the runway
- **Bank costs height** — the rate of descent and the stall speed both go up in a turn, and the turn has to be steep to be quick
- You'd land **downwind**, at a higher groundspeed, on a runway that may have traffic on it
- The **startle** and the pull to get back home make a low, slow, steep turn even more likely to end in a stall

> Below your turn-back height, **land ahead**. The runway behind you is a trap.

<!--

From your workbook: [Reality Check: The Runway Behind You](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dFVFKq3QqXo) (Air Safety Institute) or [Is the "Impossible Turn" Actually Impossible?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g9PqCUg-Y2Y) (Pilot Institute) — whichever was set. Ask the student to compare their own list of reasons with this one before revealing it.

FIM Ch 11 background: the instructor should emphasise the need to resist turning steeply near the ground to return to the aerodrome, and keep reinforcing it through the student's training. The ATSB booklet's own figures show "turning back toward the departure aerodrome" was the most common response to a partial power loss (160 of 242 occurrences) and accounted for four of the fatal accidents.

Some operators publish a minimum height above which a turn back may be considered for a particular type, aerodrome and wind, and practise it at a safe height first; some don't allow it at all in training. Check your operator's procedures, and put the figure in the safety brief. Either way, the decision is made at the holding point, not at 400 ft.

-->

# Partial power after take-off

The engine is still running — but you're not sure how much, or for how long.

- **Enough to climb or hold height?** — fly a **gentle**, balanced circuit, close in, and land as soon as possible; treat the engine as if it could stop at any moment
- **Not enough** — it's an engine failure after take-off. **Lower the nose** and land ahead
- Unsure? **Closing the throttle** turns an unknown into the engine failure you've practised

> Fly the aeroplane first. Diagnosing the engine never comes before keeping control.

<!--

The ATSB booklet from workbook topic 4 is the source for the framing — its three strategies are pre-flight planning for the particular aerodrome, a thorough pre-flight and engine ground run, and positive control throughout the turn back or forced landing. Ask the student to name them before showing this slide.

FIM Ch 11 background: the instructor should discuss partial power loss and its options when briefing EFATO, at minimum the option of closing the throttle to convert it into a complete loss of power — and partial power can be more complex than a complete loss, especially if it is intermittent.

CASA's sample syllabus doesn't assess partial power after take-off as a separate element in this lesson (it's A6.3 territory, in Lesson 17), so this is awareness rather than a flown exercise unless you choose to add one. A plan for it belongs in the safety brief.

-->

# Where are you when it stops?

The higher and closer you are, the more choice you have:

| Where in the circuit | Your best option |
|---|---|
| **Climb-out or early crosswind**, below your turn-back height | Engine failure after take-off — **land ahead** |
| **Crosswind or early downwind**, at height | The runway — or another runway — may be within reach; if not, the **best field** ahead |
| **Mid to late downwind** | Turn towards the runway **now** — a **glide approach**, cutting the corner if you need to |
| **Base or final** | You're already where a glide approach starts — **glide approach** to the runway |

> The runway is only the answer if you can **reach** it — with height to spare.

<!--

# Engine failure in the circuit — the actions

1. **Aviate** — **glide attitude**, best glide speed, **trim**
2. **Navigate** — **can I make the runway?** If yes, turn towards it now; if not, the **best field** in reach. Flap only once assured
3. **Communicate** — **MAYDAY** — the circuit traffic needs to know, too; squawk **7700** if fitted
4. **Troubleshoot** — quick checks (**carburettor heat, fuel, mixture**) only with height to spare

Then, as time permits: **fuel, ignition and master off**, doors unlatched.

> The radio matters more with traffic around — but it still comes after the aeroplane and the landing area.

<!--

# Why practise a glide approach?

- It's **how almost every engine failure in the circuit ends** — a glide to the runway, or close to it
- With no power, you can't adjust the descent with the throttle — your **judgement** of the glide is all you have
- It builds the eye you'll need for the forced landing from the cruise in **Lesson 17**

> A normal approach is flown with power to fix the errors. A glide approach is flown with **height in hand** instead.

<!--

FIM Ch 12 background: in a glide approach there is no power to adjust the rate of descent, so a high standard of judgement is needed to decide where to close the throttle; the rate and angle of descent are high; and the round-out involves a larger attitude change and has to be started earlier than an engine-assisted approach. Developing that judgement is the stated reason the glide approach is taught at all — it is the foundation for the later emergency exercises.

Underpinning knowledge A6 4(d): judging descent profiles in various configurations.

-->

---

# Flying the glide approach

1. **Late downwind** — normal checks; **carburettor heat** on before the power comes off
2. **Abeam the threshold** (or on base, when you're sure you can glide there) — **throttle closed**, nose down to **best glide speed**, **trim**
3. **Turn base** early enough to keep the runway comfortably within reach
4. Aim **a third of the way into the runway** — not the threshold
5. Only when the runway is **assured** — **flap**, and move the aim point back towards the threshold
6. **Round out earlier** and with a **bigger attitude change** than a powered approach

> Aim long, then bring it back — you can always lose height, but you can never get it back.

The NZ CAA Flight Instructor Guide's glide approach lesson (on its whiteboard, linked from the lesson page) sets the same structure: carb heat early on late downwind, throttle fully closed and the base turn started abeam the threshold at 1,000 ft, aim a third of the way in down to about 500 ft, then reduce the glide with flap, airspeed, S-turns or a sideslip, in that order, only once the answer to "can I easily make it?" is a clear yes. Check your aircraft's flight manual for its glide speed, the approach speed with flap, and whether carburettor heat is required at idle. In practice, open the throttle briefly every so often during a long glide to keep the engine warm and to check it still responds — per your operator's procedures.

# What changes the glide?

| Factor | Effect on distance over the ground |
|---|---|
| **Speed** | Best glide speed goes furthest — **faster or slower** both steepen it |
| **Wind** | Headwind **shorter** (fly a few knots faster); tailwind **longer** |
| **Weight** | Same glide **ratio** — a higher best glide speed when heavy |
| **Flap**, windmilling propeller | More drag, a **steeper** glide — save flap until assured |
| **Turns** | Each turn costs height — more with a steeper bank |

> The one lever that never helps: **raising the nose** to stretch the glide. It only makes it steeper — and slower.

# Too high, or too low?

Watch the **aim point** against a fixed reference in the windscreen — the same picture you've used for every approach since Lesson 6:

| The aim point... | You are... | So... |
|---|---|---|
| **Rises** in the windscreen | **Low** — undershooting | Turn in **towards** the runway; keep the flap **up**; choose a nearer landing area if it's still rising |
| **Sinks** in the windscreen | **High** — overshooting | **Flap** first, then a little more speed, then S-turns or a sideslip (type-permitting) |
| **Holds still** | **On profile** | Keep it there |

> Correct **early**. A small correction at 800 ft is easy; the same error at 200 ft may have no fix at all.

<!--

The order for the overshoot — flap, airspeed, S-turns, sideslip — follows the NZ CAA Flight Instructor Guide's glide approach lesson, which ranks them by effectiveness in a modern low-drag trainer. A sideslip is the least effective without flap, and some flight manuals restrict sideslipping with flap extended — check yours before teaching it.

The undershoot has fewer options, and that asymmetry is the whole reason the glide approach aims a third of the way in: there are four ways to lose height and none to gain it. Flap down on an undershoot is the classic error — it makes the glide steeper, not longer.

In the practice exercise, the instructor's fix for a real undershoot is simple: power, early — and a go-around if it isn't clearly going to work. The FIM is explicit that a badly overshooting glide approach should be abandoned too.
