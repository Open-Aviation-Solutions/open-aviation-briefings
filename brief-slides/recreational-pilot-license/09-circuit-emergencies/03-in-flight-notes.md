_CASA Recreational Pilot License (Aeroplane) — CASA Sample Syllabus Lesson 9_

# Language choices

> **"simulated engine failure"** — every time, before the throttle comes back. The word "simulated" is what tells the student the engine is fine.

> **"nose down"** — the first action, called the same way every time.

> **"aviate, navigate, communicate"** — the standing priority order, repeated every exercise.

> **"can you make it?"** — the question on every glide approach, rather than "you're low" or "you're high".

# Before starting the plane

- The daily inspection is done before the student arrives, unhurried — or, for a first-slot booking, walked together as a visible block at the start of the lesson. Today the fuel drain, the two-method fuel check and the oil check *are* the engine-failure prevention from workbook topic 1 — point that out
- Confirm the student's **fuel requirement** and fuel system management (C4.1, C4.2) and pre-flight actions (C2.1, C2.2) — assessed this lesson
- Confirm your operator's **minimum heights** for a simulated engine failure after take-off and its go-around, and whether **rejected take-offs** may be practised on today's runway
- During the start, talk through the actions for a **flooded start** and an **engine fire on start-up** (A1.1(x), introduced this lesson — not yet assessed)

# During taxi

- Check the **windsock** — direction and strength. The wind decides which way you'd turn after an engine failure after take-off, and how far you'll glide on every glide approach today
- Look at the **ground beyond the upwind end** of the runway on the way to the holding point — where would you land ahead?
- At the holding point, after the pre-take-off checks, the student gives the **safety brief** — out loud, before every take-off from here on

Example safety brief for the first take-off:

> *"Runway* ______*, wind* ______*. If the engine fails or runs rough during the take-off roll, I'll close the throttle and stop. If it fails after take-off with runway remaining, I'll land ahead on the runway. If there's no runway remaining, I'll lower the nose, glide at* ______ *kt and land ahead in* ______*. I won't turn back below* ______ *ft."*

<!--

# Rejected take-off

## Sequence
- Only with plenty of runway, at low speed early in the roll, and as your operator allows
- Brief it first: "On this take-off I'll simulate a problem on the roll — I'll say 'reject' and bring the throttle back"
- Early in the roll: partly close the throttle — "Simulated rough running — reject"
- Student: **throttle closed**, **keep straight** with rudder, **brakes** as needed, control column back
- Once stopped: talk through the shutdown and the radio call, then vacate or back-track as local procedures allow

<!--

CASA A2.2(x): recognise take-off abnormalities and take appropriate action to reject take-off (can be simulated). Because it *can* be simulated, a talk-through at the holding point is a fair substitute where the runway, traffic or operator procedures don't suit a real one. The NZ CAA Flight Instructor Guide uses a partial closing of the throttle early in the roll to simulate the failure.

During normal take-offs today, ask the student to call the full-throttle RPM and "airspeed alive" out loud early in the roll — that's the A2.2(q) monitoring that makes a rejected take-off possible.

# Engine failure after take-off — instructor demonstration

## Sequence
- Brief it on the downwind or at the holding point: "On this climb-out I'll demonstrate a simulated engine failure after take-off — you watch the sequence, then it's yours"
- Climbing out on the extended centreline, at or above the briefed minimum height: throttle closed — "Simulated engine failure"
- **Aviate** — lower the nose to the glide attitude **immediately**; settle at best glide speed
- **Navigate** — point out the landing area **ahead**, within about 30° of the nose; a small turn only if obstacles dictate
- **Communicate** — say what the MAYDAY would be, but don't transmit it
- Touch and call the shutdown, as time permits — "fuel off, ignition off, master off after the flap, doors unlatched" — without moving the controls
- At the briefed go-around height: "Going around" — full power, climb attitude, flap up as required, rejoin the circuit

<!--

CASA A6.1(c–h). Narrate every step out loud — this demonstration is the model the student repeats next. Use the same words as the theory and the pre-flight brief, so the student hears one vocabulary.

FIM Ch 11 background: demonstrate from about 500 ft; stress that the gliding attitude has to be taken very quickly; choose an area ahead with the minimum change of heading. It also asks that the conditions and terrain ahead for the demonstration be chosen so it builds confidence rather than the reverse — pick a runway and wind where the area ahead is genuinely landable.

Touch-and-call, not move: the fuel, ignition and master stay where they are on a simulated failure.

# Engine failure after take-off — student flown

## Sequence
- Repeat on later climb-outs, without warning beyond the briefing that it will happen at some point
- Student flies the sequence from memory: **nose down**, area ahead, actions as time permits
- Watch the **first second** above all — the nose must come down before anything else
- Go around at the briefed height on your call; debrief briefly on the downwind: what worked, what would you do differently?

<!--

CASA A6.1(c–h), assessed at level 2 this lesson. Prompt only if a step is missed or out of order — and only after the nose is down. The common failure is a late or partial lowering of the nose while the student's eyes go looking for a field, which is exactly the instinct the theory hook surfaced.

-->

# Engine failure in the circuit

## Sequence
- On downwind (or base), at circuit height: throttle closed — "Simulated engine failure"
- **Aviate** — glide attitude, best glide speed, **trim**
- **Navigate** — "Can you make the runway?" — turn towards it **now**; aim a third of the way in; flap only once it's assured
- **Communicate** — make a normal circuit position call if there's other traffic; simulate the MAYDAY content aloud, don't transmit it
- **Troubleshoot** — the quick checks (carburettor heat, fuel, mixture) only if there's height to spare
- Land on the runway, or go around at the briefed height if it isn't clearly going to work

<!--

CASA A6.2(t–y, h): identify the failure, control the aeroplane, perform recall actions, select a suitable landing area within gliding distance on the aerodrome or elsewhere, perform emergency procedures and land, advise ATS of situation and intentions. C3.1(r): emergency and urgency transmissions — talked through, not transmitted.

Vary the position across the circuits: abeam the threshold (comfortable), late downwind (marginal), early crosswind at height (is the runway even the answer?). The theory deck's circuit diagram shows the first two.

-->

# Glide approaches

## Sequence
- Late downwind: normal checks; **carburettor heat** on before the power comes off
- Abeam the threshold: throttle closed, glide attitude, best glide speed, **trim**; start the base turn
- Aim **a third of the way into the runway**; judge it against the aim point down to about 500 ft
- Once assured: **flap**, then move the aim point back towards the threshold
- Too high — flap, then a little speed, then S-turns or a sideslip (type-permitting); too low — turn in, flap up, and **power** if it's clearly not going to make it
- **Round out earlier**, with a bigger attitude change than a powered approach
- Clear the engine during the descent per your operator's procedures

<!--

CASA A3.3 (glide descent), A3.6(e–j) (approach descent path, aim point, stabilised approach), A4.1 (land aeroplane). FIM Ch 12 and the NZ CAA glide approach lesson both start the exercise on the downwind leg and close the throttle once the runway is within guaranteed gliding distance. The NZ lesson's later refinement — restricting the usable runway with an imaginary stop point — is a good extension once the third-in aim point is reliable.

Missed approach and missed landing recovery (A4.3, A4.4) are assessed this lesson: any glide approach that isn't working is a chance to see the go-around flown well.

# Common student errors

- **Slow to lower the nose** — eyes go looking for a field while the airspeed decays
- **Turning back**, or turning too far, after an engine failure after take-off
- **Flap too early** on a glide approach — the runway "looks fine" at 600 ft and then the aeroplane lands short
- **Raising the nose** to stretch an undershooting glide
- **Rounding out too late** after a glide approach, and arriving firmly
- **Radio first** — the call made before the aeroplane is under control

# Return to aerodrome

- A final **normal** circuit and landing — let the student finish on a powered approach after the glides
- Complete the post-flight actions and administration at shutdown

# Before you part — introduce the next lesson

Two or three minutes at the end of the debrief, no more — the point is that the student leaves knowing what is coming, and what to do before then.

**Next: Lesson 10 — Circuits — pre-solo.**

- **What it is** — consolidation, not a new skill: circuits, landings, go-arounds and missed landings, and today's simulated engine failures, all flown to the standard needed for first solo
- **The link from today** — the emergencies you flew today are part of the pre-solo standard. You'll be expected to give the safety brief, and to handle an engine failure after take-off or in the circuit, without prompting
- **Before then** — the **pre-solo examination** (booked today), and revise your aircraft's emergency checklists and local circuit procedures
