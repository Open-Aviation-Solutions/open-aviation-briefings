# Circuit Emergencies
## Pre-lesson Workbook

_CASA Recreational Pilot License (Aeroplane) — CASA Sample Syllabus Lesson 9, Pre-lesson Workbook_

Complete this before your lesson — on your own, at your own pace. For each topic: watch a video, read an article, or go and find the answer yourself, then answer the questions in the space provided. Print this out, or write your answers in a notebook. It should take about 30–45 minutes in total, and we'll check it together at the start of your pre-flight brief.

Some topics deliberately ask you to **think first**, before you're given an answer. Those are the ones worth slowing down on — reasoning it out yourself is what makes it stick when you need it.

# 1. Why engines fail — and how you stop it happening

A well-maintained engine rarely just stops. Most engine stoppages trace back to something the pilot could have caught: fuel, carburettor ice, or contamination. That makes **prevention** — the daily inspection, the fuel drain, the run-up — your first line of defence.

**Read:**
- [Engine failure after take-off](https://www.aviation.govt.nz/licensing-and-certification/pilots/flight-training/flight-instructor-guide/engine-failure-after-take-off/) — NZ CAA Flight Instructor Guide. Read the "Common causes and their prevention" part of the Considerations section only — we'll come back to the rest live.

**Watch (pick one):**
- [Filler cap fault leads to fuel starvation and engine failure](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JOh56fqS9s0) — ATSB, about 4½ minutes. A real Australian occurrence, worked through by the investigators.
- [Fuel Exhaustion on @CASABriefing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SkxPDvHilWE) — ATSB, about a minute. Short and blunt about the single most avoidable cause.

**Answer:**

1. Name four common causes of an engine stoppage in a light aircraft: ________________________________

2. For each cause above, name the pre-flight or run-up check that would most likely catch it: ________________________________

3. What's the difference between fuel **starvation** and fuel **exhaustion**? ________________________________

4. Food for thought: why do you think the page says mechanical failure is *not* the most common cause? ________________________________

# 2. Abnormal take-offs and the rejected take-off

Not every problem waits until you're airborne. Low RPM, sluggish acceleration, rough running or an open door can all show up during the take-off roll — and on the ground the answer is simple, as long as you decide quickly.

**Watch (pick one):**
- [Takeoff Emergencies](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aTWMZqFTmaM) — Gold Seal Flight Training, about 8½ minutes. Clear graphics on what can go wrong on the roll and just after lift-off.
- [Takeoff Emergencies — Runway Remaining](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UoMJcOrgcEw) — Gold Seal Flight Training, about 6 minutes. The case where you're airborne but there's still runway ahead.
- [Abort! Demonstrating a Rejected Takeoff in Real Time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OLNNLXA14Zg) — MzeroA Flight Training, about 10½ minutes. A rejected take-off flown and talked through from the cockpit.

**Research:**
- Look up the **"50/70 rule"** for take-off (a search engine or an AI assistant will find it). Note what it says, and where you found it.

**Answer:**

1. Name three things you might notice during the take-off roll that should make you reject the take-off: ________________________________

2. What are the first two actions of a rejected take-off? ________________________________

3. What does the 50/70 rule say, and how could you use it on your home runway? ________________________________

4. If you've just lifted off and there is still plenty of runway ahead, what should you do if the engine fails? ________________________________

# 3. The turn back

After an engine failure shortly after take-off, the runway you just left seems like the obvious place to land. It's one of the most-discussed decisions in light aviation, and the answer in training is almost always the same.

**Think first:**
- Before you watch anything: if the engine stopped at 300 ft on climb-out, why might turning back to the runway be harder than it looks? Write down every reason you can think of.

**Watch (pick one):**
- [Reality Check: The Runway Behind You](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dFVFKq3QqXo) — Air Safety Institute, about 9½ minutes. Real test flights of the turn back, and what the startle factor does to the numbers.
- [Is the "Impossible Turn" Actually Impossible?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g9PqCUg-Y2Y) — Pilot Institute, about 7½ minutes. The aerodynamics and the geometry, explained.

**Answer:**

1. Your own reasons first — why might a turn back be harder than it looks? ________________________________

2. After watching: name two reasons from the video that you hadn't thought of: ________________________________

3. Why isn't a 180° turn enough to line back up with the runway? ________________________________

4. What happens to the stall speed and the rate of descent as you steepen the bank in a glide? ________________________________

# 4. Partial power after take-off

An engine that keeps running but won't give full power is more likely than one that stops completely — and it leaves you with a decision to make, which is what makes it hard.

**Read:**
- [Avoidable Accidents No. 3 — Managing partial power loss after take-off in single-engine aircraft](https://www.atsb.gov.au/publications/avoidable-accidents/2022/aviation/avoidable-accidents-no-3-managing-partial-power-loss) — ATSB. Read the introduction and the key messages at minimum; the case studies are worth it if you have time.

**Watch (optional, for more depth):**
- [Partial Power Loss: The Emergency We Don't Train For](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9ldAL-GOxRY) — Flight Training Australia, about 22 minutes.

**Answer:**

1. According to the ATSB, how much more likely is a partial power loss after take-off than a complete engine failure? ________________________________

2. List the three strategies the ATSB recommends: ________________________________

3. Which action after a partial power loss led to the most occurrences, and why do you think pilots chose it? ________________________________

4. Food for thought: if a partial power loss leaves you unsure whether the engine will keep running, what could you do to turn it into a situation you've already practised? ________________________________

# 5. How far will it glide?

With the engine gone, your height is your only energy — and how far it takes you depends on the speed you fly, the wind, and how the aeroplane is configured. Knowing the factors lets you judge, rather than guess, whether you'll make the runway.

**Watch (pick one):**
- [How far can airplane glide?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=klqXonOpPB0) — Flight Club, about 4½ minutes. Glide ratio and best glide speed, simply explained.
- [CASA RPL PPL Tutorials #4 Glide Performance Wind Weight Configuration](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N9XfZb7iuJw) — Evionica, under 2 minutes. Written for the Australian RPL/PPL syllabus.

**Answer:**

1. Look up the **best glide speed** for the aircraft you're training in, and where in the flight manual you found it: ________________________________

2. With a glide ratio of 10:1, how far could you glide from a 1,000 ft circuit (in still air)? Give it in metres or nautical miles: ________________________________

3. Would a headwind make that distance longer or shorter over the ground? What about a tailwind? ________________________________

4. How does extending flap change the glide — and so when would you choose to use it? ________________________________

5. Food for thought: a heavier aeroplane glides at the same *ratio* as a lighter one, but at a different speed. Faster or slower? ________________________________

# Bring this to your lesson

- Bring your completed answers (printed, or your notebook) to the pre-flight brief
- We'll check through them together and answer anything that didn't make sense — especially your own list of reasons not to turn back
- From there, the live theory brief builds on this: the rejected take-off, your pre-take-off safety brief, the engine failure after take-off actions, what changes when the engine fails elsewhere in the circuit, and flying and judging a glide approach

> The time to decide what you'll do if the engine fails on take-off is **before** you open the throttle — not after. This workbook is where that planning starts.
